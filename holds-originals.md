## dq-phys-cvs-009 — valve state during diastole
- Stem: During normal diastole, the state of the heart valves is that the:
- Key: D — mitral and tricuspid valves are open.
- Concern: This is correct during ventricular filling, but all four valves are closed during early isovolumetric relaxation, also a normal part of ventricular diastole. The unqualified stem admits another valid phase-specific answer. Recommend qualifying the stem as the ventricular filling phase during independent review; no question fields or explanation changed.
- Reference: Guyton and Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology, cardiac cycle (isovolumic relaxation and ventricular filling); textbook section identified from standard physiology, exact edition/page not independently checked.

## dq-phys-cvs-040 — giant versus cannon a waves
- Stem: A giant 'a' wave in the jugular venous pulse is characteristically seen in:
- Key: E — heart block.
- Concern: Intermittent cannon a waves occur with AV dissociation (e.g. complete heart block), when the right atrium contracts against a closed tricuspid valve. Giant a waves generally describe increased resistance to right atrial emptying, e.g. tricuspid stenosis or pulmonary hypertension. Not every degree of heart block produces cannon waves. Recommend specifying cannon waves and complete AV block; original explanation/key untouched.
- Reference: standard clinical cardiovascular examination and JVP physiology (Bates and Guyton & Hall; edition/page not independently checked).

## dq-phys-cvs-046 — exercise and afterload
- Stem: During exercise, cardiac output rises partly because afterload falls as a result of:
- Key: A — muscle vasodilatation.
- Concern: Active muscle vasodilatation lowers systemic vascular resistance during dynamic exercise, but systolic arterial pressure often rises and left ventricular afterload (wall stress) is not interchangeable with peripheral resistance. Afterload is not necessarily lower in every exercise state. Recommend qualifying the question as dynamic exercise and asking about reduced systemic vascular resistance rather than assuming a fall in afterload. Original explanation/key untouched.
- Reference: Guyton & Hall, exercise physiology and regulation of cardiac output; edition/page not independently checked.

- Stem: The central venous pressure, measured at the entrance to the right atrium, is normally about:
- Key: E — 4-6 mmHg.
- Concern: Clinical normal CVP depends on measurement reference, position and respiratory phase; common clinical ranges include approximately 2–6 mmHg, while classical Guyton physiology describes mean right atrial pressure near 0 mmHg. Both 0 mmHg and 4–6 mmHg appear among the options. The existing explanation adopts the course convention, but the exact lecture slide has not been independently verified. Recommend confirming the intended convention before rewriting. Original explanation/key untouched.
- Reference: Guyton & Hall, venous return and right atrial pressure; standard clinical CVP measurement conventions; exact edition/page not independently checked.

## dq-phys-cvs-079 — sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator fibres
- Stem: Sympathetic vasodilator fibres to vessels in skeletal muscle are unusual in that they are:
- Key: D — cholinergic.
- Concern: Classical sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator pathways are described in some animal species; evidence for a functionally important equivalent in human skeletal muscle is limited. Human exercise hyperaemia depends strongly on local metabolic and endothelial mechanisms. The existing note also generalises this pathway to the liver. Confirm the course's species/context convention before teaching it as a general human mechanism. Original explanation and key untouched.
- Reference: Guyton & Hall, nervous regulation of circulation; standard human exercise physiology (exact edition/page not independently checked).

## dq-phys-cvs-088 — direct medullary vasomotor stimulation
- Stem: The vasomotor centre is directly stimulated by:
- Key: C — hypoxia and hypercapnia.
- Concern: Severe cerebral ischaemia can elicit a central pressor response involving local CO2/H+ accumulation and hypoxia, but systemic hypoxaemia also acts through peripheral chemoreceptor afferents, and severe/prolonged hypoxia may depress central neurons. The word "directly" requires context. Confirm whether the lecture refers specifically to the CNS ischaemic response before revising. Original explanation and key untouched.
- Reference: Guyton & Hall, CNS ischaemic response and arterial chemoreflexes (exact edition/page not independently checked).

## dq-phys-cvs-091 — positive acceleration and visual blackout
- Stem: During exposure to high positive (+Gz) acceleration, loss of vision ('black-out') occurs because of:
- Key: A — reduced cerebral perfusion.
- Concern: Positive acceleration reduces perfusion pressure at the head, but visual blackout specifically reflects inadequate retinal perfusion, which can precede cerebral hypoperfusion severe enough to cause loss of consciousness. The keyed option is the closest among those supplied, but not the precise mechanism for visual blackout. Recommend specifying retinal hypoperfusion or changing the endpoint to loss of consciousness during independent review. Original explanation and key untouched.
- Reference: standard aviation/acceleration physiology; Guyton & Hall, aviation and space physiology (exact edition/page not independently checked).

## dq-phys-cvs-104 — lymphatic return fraction
- Stem: Of the fluid filtered out of the capillaries each day, the proportion returned to the circulation by the lymphatics is about:
- Key: D — 15%.
- Concern: The classic teaching model attributes most filtered fluid to sustained venous-end capillary reabsorption, with about 10–15% returned by lymphatics. The revised Starling principle indicates that sustained reabsorption is uncommon in most systemic microvascular beds at steady state and lymphatic drainage returns most net filtered fluid. The keyed percentage is therefore model-dependent and should not be taught as a general modern fact. Original explanation/key unchanged pending course review.
- Reference: Levick and Michel, Microvascular fluid exchange and the revised Starling principle, Cardiovascular Research (2010); exact text not independently retrieved.

## dq-phys-cvs-108 — lymphatic venous termination
- Stem: The lymphatic vessels ultimately return lymph to the bloodstream at the:
- Key: C — junction of the subclavian veins.
- Concern: The usual lymphatic duct termination sites are the ipsilateral venous angles, where internal jugular and subclavian veins join, with anatomical variation. The two subclavian veins do not directly form a junction with each other. The keyed option appears intended to describe the venous angles but is anatomically imprecise. Original explanation/key unchanged.
- Reference: standard gross anatomy of thoracic duct and right lymphatic drainage (Moore/Gray's; exact edition/page not checked).

## dq-phys-cvs-113 — coronary stenosis threshold for exertional angina
- Stem: Angina pectoris on exertion typically occurs when a coronary artery is narrowed by about:
- Key: C — 75%.
- Concern: The stem does not specify diameter versus cross-sectional area stenosis, and symptoms depend on lesion physiology, coronary reserve, microvascular function and myocardial demand. A 75% area reduction corresponds to 50% diameter reduction in a circular lumen, both present as options. Confirm the lecture convention; do not imply a universal symptom threshold. Original explanation/key unchanged.
